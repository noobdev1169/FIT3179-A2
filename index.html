1

How many?

1.24 million

students in Years 1–10 missed more than a day of school a fortnight in the first half of 2025: almost 4 in 10.

That is around 400,000 more than in 2019, before COVID-19. In 2022 it was half of all students.

The national attendance rate still looks high, at 88.8%. But that average hides how many students regularly miss school, and who they are.

How attendance is measured

Attendance rate
The share of school days that students attended in Semester 1. In 2025 it was 88.8%, so students missed about 1 day in 9.
Attendance level
The share of students who attended at least 90% of days. In 2025 it was 62.1%.
Missing out (this page)
Everyone else: students who missed more than 1 day in 10, about a day a fortnight. In 2025, 37.9% (100 − 62.1).
Percentage points
The plain difference between two percentages. From 26.9% in 2019 to 37.9% in 2025 is a rise of 11.0 points.
2

When?

Absence was already creeping up before COVID-19. In 2022, the worst year in a decade of national records, half of all students missed more than a day a fortnight. Attendance has improved since, but in 2025 every year level was still missing more school than in 2019.

The pattern by age barely changes: every year, absence jumps in Year 8 and keeps rising to Year 10.

50.1%

of students missed more than a day a fortnight in 2022, the worst year since national records of this measure began in 2015.

3

Where?

The further students live from a major city, the more school they miss. In 2025, just over a third of students in major cities missed more than a day a fortnight (35.2%), compared with three in four in very remote areas (74.7%).

But a map coloured by area exaggerates the outback, where few people live. Sizing each area by its students tells the other half of the story: most students who miss out live in cities.

1.7%

of students live in remote and very remote Australia, which covers 86% of the land. That is why this section uses two maps.

Who?

The widest gaps are between Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students and other students, and they are widest in remote and very remote areas. Most students who miss out go to government (public) schools. But that is largely about who those schools teach: compare schools serving similar communities, and the gaps between sectors shrink.

88%

of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students in very remote areas missed out in 2025, against 48% of non-Indigenous students there.

What is ICSEA?

The Index of Community Socio-Educational Advantage is ACARA’s measure of the community a school serves. It is built mainly from parents’ jobs and education, and also takes into account how remote the school is and its share of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students. The national median is 1000.

ACARA groups schools into four quarters by ICSEA, from the least to the most advantaged.

Because ICSEA includes remoteness and Indigenous status, these factors overlap. This page doesn’t treat them as separate causes.

Up close: NSW public schools

New South Wales publishes attendance for each of its public schools, so here the story can be followed school by school. Attendance is recovering: most schools improved between 2022 and 2024, and the typical (median) school rose from 86.0% to 88.8%.

But schools serving less advantaged communities still have lower attendance, and they differ far more from one another. Click a group in the chart below to see where its schools are on the maps.

of NSW public schools improved their attendance between 2022 and 2024: 1,810 of 2,099 schools.

How to read the boxes

Each row is a group of schools. The line covers most schools: all but the top and bottom 10%. The box covers the middle half. The black tick is the typical (median) school: half the schools are above it and half below.

The box’s colour shows the days missed by the typical school, in the same colours as the maps.

What makes the difference?

Put the comparisons from this page side by side, and a clear order appears. Where students live and how advantaged their school’s community is make the biggest differences. Whether a student is a boy or a girl makes almost none.

39.5 points

is the gap between very remote areas and major cities, the largest on this page. Between boys and girls it is 1.0 point.

In short

  • Almost 4 in 10 students missed more than a day of school a fortnight in 2025, about 1.24 million, up from about 1 in 4 before COVID-19.
  • Absence peaked in 2022 and has only partly recovered. Every year it jumps in Year 8.
  • It rises with remoteness, and the gaps for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students are widest in very remote areas. But most students who miss out live in cities.
  • Compared like with like, community advantage matters much more than school sector.

What this data can’t tell us

The figures count days missed, not why they were missed. They don’t separate illness from other reasons, and they can’t show what would raise attendance.
